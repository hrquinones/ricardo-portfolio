## Context

Personal Pay's card platform started as a single, tightly-coupled service —
a card "microlith" handling issuance, tracking, and related flows together.

## Problem

As the card product surface grew, the microlith made it harder to evolve
individual capabilities independently, and the frontend had no clear
boundary of responsibility per product.

## Constraints

Changes had to ship without disrupting the live onboarding and tracking
flows the microlith already supported, and multiple product teams needed to
build on top of the platform independently.

## Role

Designed the target architecture and led the migration as Senior Backend
Engineer and Tech Lead.

## Approach

Split the microlith into a microservices structure, and in parallel
redesigned the frontend architecture around a Backend for Frontend (BFF) per
product, establishing service contracts with a higher level of abstraction
between frontend and backend teams.

## Architecture

- A microservices backend replacing the original card microlith.
- A per-product BFF layer separating frontend concerns from backend service
  contracts.
- Reusable UI components shared across product teams, with the codebase
  restructured toward a modular, product-based organization aimed at
  eventually evolving into microfrontends.
- A dedicated microservice for card-shipment tracking, integrating a
  streaming API and SQS to manage shipment events with configurable
  response mapping for end users.

## Key technical decisions

- Adopted a BFF-per-product pattern instead of a single shared frontend
  contract, trading some duplication for team-level independence.
- Structured the codebase by product module rather than by technical layer,
  as a deliberate step toward microfrontends rather than a full rewrite.

## Result

The card platform moved from a single microlith to an independently
evolvable microservices architecture, with reusable UI components adopted by
other teams and a shipment-tracking microservice built on the new structure.

## Lessons learned

Splitting a monolith pays off fastest when the frontend boundary moves with
it. Redesigning the BFF alongside the backend, rather than after, avoided
leaving the frontend coupled to the old service shape.
